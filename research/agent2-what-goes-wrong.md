# What Goes Wrong — Clause Types Ranked

Ranked by how often and how severely they show up in complaints/evidence found (8 searches, budget respected).

## 1. Auto-renewal / negative-option billing
Burns people because signup is one click, cancellation is deliberately hard (phone-only, in-person, mail, delay windows), and charges continue after the person believes they've left.
- FTC receives "nearly 70 complaints a day on average" about subscription cancellation difficulty, up from 42/day in 2021. Example: Vanessa Ribeiro authorized a single-month Gold's Gym membership, was charged on a recurring basis without authorization, was denied phone cancellation, and hit a 30-day processing delay before cancellation took effect. This evidence pattern is why the FTC built the "click-to-cancel" rule (effective April 2025).
- Source: https://www.customerexperiencedive.com/news/ftc-click-to-cancel-rule-customer-experience/730334/

## 2. Fee escalators / unilateral price increases mid-contract
Burns people because they believe they locked in a price, then get hit with large increases they didn't negotiate, sometimes justified by unilateral ToS changes.
- Example cited: a customer signed an annual SaaS contract believed to lock in pricing for 12 months; the vendor emailed customers that it had updated its Terms of Service and prices were going up 40% effective a fixed date — a jump that would cost one customer with 85 seats an extra $18,000/year. Escalator clauses of 3-10% built into contracts are described as now standard rather than negotiated at renewal.
- Source: https://terms.law/forum/thread/saas-vendor-changed-tos-raise-prices-midcontract.html (as summarized/cited via search) — see also industry context at https://bunny.com/blog/inflationary-increases-in-saas-contracts-great-idea-hard-reality/

## 3. Arbitration clauses + class-action waivers
Burns people because they unknowingly forfeit the right to sue or join a class, forcing individual, often uneconomical, private arbitration — which is how companies avoid accountability at scale.
- Example: Mary Botorff signed a U-Haul rental contract containing an arbitration clause and class-action ban; separately, West Covina Toyota successfully compelled arbitration and had a lawsuit dismissed based on a class-action-waiver sales contract. Analysis notes these clauses mean "when companies wrong thousands or millions of people, each victim must fight alone."
- Source: https://compens.ai/en/articles/forced-arbitration-class-action-waiver-rights-2025

## 4. IP assignment / "work for hire" clauses (freelance contracts)
Burns freelancers because a single clause can transfer ownership of their creative/technical output — including work outside the legal definition of "work for hire" — costing them their portfolio and future revenue.
- "IP assignment happens to freelancers every day, and it is almost always the result of a single clause... It is the difference between building a career on your body of work and starting from scratch with every new project." Legal note: under the U.S. Copyright Act, a "work for hire" label is only valid for 9 statutory categories — logos, websites, illustrations, photography, and marketing copy are NOT among them, meaning many freelancer "work for hire" clauses are legally meaningless but still trigger disputes/litigation risk.
- Source: https://www.usepact.org/blog/post/freelancer-ip-rights-guide

## 5. Indemnification clauses (uncapped)
Burns small businesses/contractors because broad "any and all claims" language shifts financial risk from the more powerful party, and without a cap a single incident can bankrupt the smaller party — even for harm caused by the other side's negligence.
- "There's a risk of uncapped liability—without limits, one bad situation could bankrupt your business... A subcontractor who agrees to indemnify a general contractor for 'any and all claims arising out of the project' might still pay for medical expenses if an accident happens because of the general contractor's oversight."
- Source: https://mhslaw.com/the-hidden-danger-in-your-contracts-indemnification-clauses-that-can-sink-a-small-business/

## 6. Liability caps / exclusion of consequential damages (SaaS)
Burns small-business customers because when a vendor's failure causes data loss or downtime, the standard limitation-of-liability clause caps recovery at fees paid (often 12 months) and explicitly excludes "consequential" damages like lost revenue or lost data — the exact harm most likely to occur.
- "Lost data is often specified in the list of excluded damages... a customer's data loss might be a direct damage, but the revenue they lost because they could not access that data is a consequential damage. Most terms of service exclude consequential damages entirely because they can be enormous and unpredictable."
- Source: https://www.pactdraft.ai/blog/terms-of-service-liability-limitation (see also https://www.cloudnuro.ai/blog/saas-liability-cap)

## 7. Non-compete clauses in freelance/contractor agreements
Burns independent workers because non-competes are frequently included in contractor agreements even though contractors are not employees, effectively locking freelancers out of their own profession and chilling them from taking other work out of fear, even when the clause may be legally unenforceable.
- "Young creators have been so intimidated by non-compete clauses in their contracts that they turned down work repeatedly for fear of violating the agreement, and some even asked the client's permission to take on other work, with clients refusing to let creators take many jobs even after not having hired them in five years or more." Courts have also flagged non-competes in contractor agreements as evidence of employee misclassification.
- Source: https://fleximize.com/articles/001570/non-compete-clauses-for-freelancers-part-1 (misclassification point via https://worksuite.com/resources/insights/independent-contractor-agreements)

## 8. Unilateral/landlord-favorable termination clauses (leases)
Burns tenants because commercial and some residential leases grant the landlord one-sided rights to terminate (e.g., for demolition/rehab) while tenants must rely on statutory notice and eviction protections that vary heavily by state; enforceability of these clauses is a recurring point of dispute.
- "Landlords may have the right to terminate a lease if they decide to demolish or rehabilitate the building, provided they give notice not later than six months prior to the proposed termination date... Unilateral termination clauses are often found in commercial leases and are less common in residential agreements, which typically have stronger tenant protections under state law."
- Source: https://www.lowndes-law.com/newsroom/insights/when-are-unilateral-termination-rights-in-a-commercial-lease-enforceable
